# Double-Coated Dog Grooming Guide

Here is the guide. It follows the current professional order for double coats: light pre-brush, bath, condition, high-velocity blowout, then the real brushing on a clean, dry coat.

## Before you start

- **Never shave her.** Shaving a double coat damages the guard hairs, ruins insulation, and the coat often grows back wrong. Deshedding removes dead undercoat only.
- **Pick the location.** The blowout throws out an astonishing amount of fur and water mist. Do it outside on a leash, or in a bathroom with the curtain closed. Wear a mask and old clothes.
- **Do a hands-on check.** Run your fingers through to the skin everywhere. Note any mats, hot spots, ticks, or sore skin. Problem zones on Bernese-type coats: behind the ears, under the collar, armpits, the "britches" on the back of the thighs, tail base, and the leg feathering.
- **Desensitize her to the dryer.** Run it for a few minutes across the room during a treat session a day or two beforehand. Then let it blow on her rear end briefly. Never point it at her face on the first day.
- **Timebox.** A full deshed on a heavy coat takes 2 to 3 hours. Split it over two days if she gets tired.

## The full groom, step by step

**1. Light pre-brush (10 minutes).**
Do a quick pass with the slicker to remove leaves, loose surface hair, and any big tangles. Water tightens mats, so break up anything obvious now. Do not try to deshed fully yet. Brushing a dirty coat dulls tools and fights you.

**2. Handle mats before water.**
Rub a small amount of coconut oil into your fingers, work it into the mat, then pick it apart from the edges with the coarse end of the comb, holding the base of the fur against the skin so you don't pull. If a mat is tight against the skin, do not cut it with scissors pointed inward. Slide the comb flat between the mat and the skin as a shield, then cut on the outside of the comb. If it is a large pelted area, that is a groomer or vet job.

**3. Soak to the skin (5 to 10 minutes).**
Use lukewarm water. A dense double coat repels water at first. Part the fur with your hand as you spray so water reaches the skin. She is not wet until you can feel water on the skin down her back, chest, and thighs.

**4. Shampoo twice.**
Dilute the shampoo in a jug of warm water so it spreads through dense fur. Work it down to the skin with your fingertips against the lay of the hair. Rinse. The first wash lifts dirt and oil, the second actually cleans. Keep it out of eyes and ears.

**5. Rinse until you are sure, then rinse again.**
Leftover shampoo in undercoat is the number one cause of itching and dandruff after a home bath. Water should run completely clear and the coat should feel squeaky, not slick.

**6. Condition and loosen the undercoat.**
Dilute the conditioner, saturate the whole coat, and massage to the skin. Let it sit 3 to 5 minutes. While it sits, run the slicker gently through the coat, especially the britches and neck. Conditioner smooths the hair cuticle so dead undercoat slides out instead of tearing. Rinse completely.

**7. Towel blot, don't rub.**
Squeeze water out of the feathering and press the towel into the coat. Rubbing tangles long fur.

**8. High-velocity blowout.**
This step removes more undercoat than any brush.

- Start at the rear and work forward so she gets used to the noise.
- Hold the nozzle a few inches from the coat, aimed roughly straight at it, so the fur fans out in a "sunburst" around the skin.
- **Hold it still and move it slowly.** Waving it back and forth whips long feathering into knots.
- On the long feathering, ears, and tail, pull the nozzle back further or remove the narrow tip.
- Work in the direction the hair lies once the area is mostly dry.
- **Never** blast the face, inside the ears, eyes, or genitals. Do the head last with the dryer on low or with no nozzle, aiming down the neck, or just towel it. Cover the ear openings with your thumb when working near them.
- Dead undercoat will fly out in clouds. Pause every few minutes and brush the loosened fur out with the slicker, then keep drying.
- **Dry to the skin.** Damp undercoat trapped against the skin causes hot spots and a musty smell. Part the fur in the armpits, behind the ears, under the tail, and along the spine and feel for damp with your fingertips.
- Most forced-air dryers make warm air from the motor alone. If yours has a heat setting, use the lowest one.

**9. Line brushing (the real deshedding).**
Now that the coat is clean and dry, work in layers.

- Start at a back leg. With one hand, push the fur up against the grain so you see a line of skin.
- With the slicker, brush the layer just below that line, from skin to tip, in the direction of growth. Use light pressure and a flick of the wrist. "Brush the hair, not the skin." Pressing hard causes brush burn, a raw red rash.
- Drop your hand about an inch to expose the next line and repeat. Work up the leg, across the body, up the neck, and down the tail.
- Spend extra time in the problem zones listed above.
- If the slicker keeps filling with soft grey fluff, keep going. When it comes out nearly empty, that area is done.

**10. Comb check.**
Run the coarse end of the metal comb through every area, skin to tip. It must pass without catching. Where it snags, go back to the slicker. Then do the feathering, ears, and tail with the fine end. The comb is your quality check. The coat is only done when the fine teeth glide.

**11. Scissor tidy (minimal, safety first).**
Household scissors are sharp and pointed, so be conservative.

- **Paw pads:** with her paw held firmly, lay the scissors flat against the pads and cut the fur level with the pads. Do not cut between the pads or dig between toes. Trim the "slippers" of fur around the outside edge of each foot so it looks like a neat paw.
- **Hocks:** comb the fur on the back of the lower rear legs straight out and trim it into a tidy line. Cut with the scissor tips pointing away from the leg.
- **Sanitary area:** only if fur is soiled or trapping mess. Take small snips with the comb between scissors and skin. Leave length, do not shave down.
- **Ear edges:** trim only the wispy flyaway hairs on the edges. Do not thin the body coat or feathering with scissors. That is the Bernese look and it protects her.

**12. Finish with coconut oil, sparingly.**
Melt a pea-sized amount between your palms and stroke it over the feathering and tail to cut static and add shine. Rub a small amount into dry paw pads, elbows, and the nose. Do not coat the body fur. Too much clogs pores, attracts dirt, and makes her lick. Once or twice a week at most. Vets caution against feeding it in large amounts because of weight gain and pancreatitis risk, so if you want to feed it, ask your vet and keep it to a teaspoon or less.

## Maintenance and safety

- **Brush 2 to 3 times a week**, 10 to 15 minutes of line brushing on the problem zones. Daily during the spring and fall coat blow.
- **Bathe every 6 to 8 weeks** or when dirty. Over-bathing strips oils. Do the full blowout at the start of each seasonal shed.
- **Watch for brush burn.** Stop if the skin turns pink.
- **Never leave her tethered in the tub or on a table unattended.**
- **Stop and see a vet** if you find hot spots, bald patches, or heavy dandruff that a good rinse didn't fix.
- **Call a groomer** for pelted mats, or if she panics at the dryer. A single professional blowout at the start of shedding season plus your weekly brushing is the best cost-to-result trade.
